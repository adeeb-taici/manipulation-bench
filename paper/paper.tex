\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% =========================================================================

Frontier language models are increasingly deployed in high-trust settings where their outputs influence human decisions and beliefs. Examples include customer-facing sales systems, decision-support tools, and negotiation agents. This raises concerns about their capacity to manipulate counterparties and the manipulative tactics they tend toward. This is especially concerning because frontier models deploy manipulative cues at non-trivial rates even when not explicitly prompted to do so \citep{akbulut2026evaluating} and match or exceed incentivized human persuaders on belief-change tasks \citep{hackenburg2025levers,lin2025persuading}.

Existing benchmarks predominantly measure \emph{whether} a model manipulates within fixed or highly related settings. Static honesty benchmarks vary the test items but fix the prompt format and elicitation context; scheming evaluations vary goal pressure but typically within a single agentic scenario family; multi-agent benchmarks vary the strategic environment but rarely cross prompt-level axes within environments. This results in a fragmented view of a model's propensity and capability to manipulate or deceive: a model may appear honest in one condition and manipulative in another, with no principled account of how its behavior interpolates between them.

We evaluate manipulative behavior across 12{,}510 scenarios spanning five multi-agent environments (Bargaining, Debate, Village Commons, Sales, and Committee) crossed with three experimental axes (Framing, Incentive, and Difficulty). Our headline findings are threefold. First, the dominant driver of manipulation is task-dependent: in tasks where the manipulator's lever is a single-step behavioral commitment (such as stating a number or contributing to a pool), prompt framing dominates outcomes; in tasks where the manipulator constructs claims that have to fit alongside an underlying truth, the substrate dominates. Second, model rankings do not transfer across tasks (mean Spearman correlation 0.055 across the ten task-pairs), arguing against the existence of a stable manipulation-propensity trait. Third, verbal incentives are inert in three of five tasks, with measurable effects only emerging when incentives introduce new reward structures.

Additionally, our contributions are threefold. First, we provide evidence that manipulation behavior is task-dependent rather than a stable model-level trait, and we characterize the structure of that dependence through a parametric evaluation across five environments and three axes. Second, we report a substantively important null result on the incentive axis, showing that verbal performance incentives common in production AI systems are inert in the absence of structural integration with the agent's reward function. Third, we release our multi-agent evaluation framework built on top of Inspect, including environment designs, deterministic rule-based scorers, and pre-registered prediction outcomes for benchmark cells that \citet{shi2026hallucination} identifies as critical gaps in the existing literature, enabling direct replication and extension to new environments or models.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
% =========================================================================

We define manipulation as a subset of persuasion characterized by epistemic subversion, following \citet{akbulut2026evaluating}. A model manipulates when it attempts to influence a target by distorting, withholding, or structuring information in ways that undermine the target's ability to make an informed decision. This definition focuses on observable behavior rather than inferred intent.

We distinguish manipulation from adjacent concepts. Rational persuasion appeals to verifiable evidence and preserves user autonomy, while coercion operates through explicit constraints on available actions. Deception, defined as the inducement of false beliefs \citep{park2024ai}, is one mechanism of manipulation but not the only one. Models can manipulate without making false statements, for example through guilt-tripping or motivated framing. \citet{carroll2023characterizing} characterize manipulation along four dimensions (\emph{intent}, \emph{covertness}, \emph{incentive}, and \emph{harm}) that further sharpen this distinction.

The economics of strategic communication identifies several mechanisms by which a sender can mislead a receiver. Two are directly relevant to our environments. \citet{milgrom1981good} and the disclosure tradition study \emph{selective revelation} of verifiable evidence, where withholding rather than fabricating is the operative mechanism. This is the central mechanism in our Sales environment, where the manipulator has full access to a structured product spec and chooses which fields to surface. \citet{rogers2017artful} introduce \emph{paltering}, the active use of literally true statements to convey a misleading impression, distinct from both lying by commission and lying by omission. Paltering is the behaviorally consequential category that purely-truth-based detection misses, and it appears across our Bargaining, Sales, and Committee environments where a manipulator can truthfully state one fact while strategically omitting another.

We adopt the taxonomy of \citet{shi2026hallucination}, which classifies manipulation by goal-directedness, object, and mechanism, with a cross-cutting dimension distinguishing the audience of the deception (e.g., a user, developer, or monitor). Their analysis of fifty benchmarks finds that existing evaluations are heavily concentrated on fabrication, with limited coverage of omission and pragmatic distortion. Our design addresses this imbalance by varying task structure and information constraints across environments.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}
% =========================================================================

\subsection{Static and single-turn deception benchmarks}
\label{subsec:static-benchmarks}

A first generation of LLM honesty benchmarks evaluates factual fabrication in single-turn QA: TruthfulQA \citep{lin2022truthfulqa}, HaluEval \citep{li2023halueval}, FActScore \citep{min2023factscore}, HalluLens \citep{bang2025hallulens}, and SimpleQA \citep{wei2024simpleqa}. Sycophancy benchmarks \citep{sharma2024towards,perez2023discovering,wei2023simple} extend this to belief-and-uncertainty fabrication under social pressure. These benchmarks are well-developed but operate in a narrow slice of the \citet{shi2026hallucination} taxonomy, almost exclusively user-directed fabrication of world or belief claims.

\subsection{Strategic-deception and scheming evaluations}
\label{subsec:strategic-benchmarks}

A second wave directly probes goal-directed deception. MACHIAVELLI \citep{pan2023rewards} evaluates power-seeking, deception, and harm across 134 text-based games. Apollo Research's in-context scheming evaluations \citep{meinke2024frontier} vary goal pressure across six agentic scenarios using a three-condition incentive protocol (Strong Goal Nudge / No Nudge / No Goal); this is the closest structural template to our design and supplies our incentive-axis anchor. Sandbagging evaluations \citep{vanderweij2024ai,tice2024noise,benton2024sabotage} target capability-concealment, while alignment faking \citep{greenblatt2024alignment} and sleeper agents \citep{hubinger2024sleeper} target deception of training and evaluation processes. DeceptionBench \citep{ji2025mitigating,huang2025deceptionbench} and MASK \citep{ren2025mask} systematize strategic-deception evaluation across categories including sycophancy, evasion, and pressure-induced lying; MASK is also notable for explicitly disentangling capability from honesty, a separation we obtain by construction through ground-truth product specs (Sales) and cold-panel-validated slates (Committee). DarkBench \citep{kran2025darkbench} and PersuSafety \citep{liu2025llm} characterize manipulative design patterns and unethical persuasion strategies in single-model settings.

\subsection{Multi-agent and game-theoretic environments}
\label{subsec:multiagent-benchmarks}

Closest to our design are evaluations situating LLMs in multi-agent strategic environments. CICERO \citep{bakhtin2022human} and WelfareDiplomacy \citep{mukobi2023welfare} study deception in Diplomacy. GovSim \citep{piatti2024cooperate} places LLMs in commons-dilemma resource-extraction games and finds 43 of 45 frontier-model-scenario runs collapse to overuse, motivating our village-commons design. NegotiationArena \citep{bianchi2024well} benchmarks LLM negotiation; the CaSiNo dataset \citep{chawla2021casino} supplies a strategy-utterance taxonomy applicable to our bargaining transcripts. WOLF \citep{agarwal2025wolf} and OpenDeception \citep{wu2026opendeception} add multi-agent social-deduction settings.

For debate, the scalable-oversight tradition \citep{khan2024debating,kenton2024scalable} establishes that more-persuasive debaters increase judge accuracy when both debaters advocate adversarially. Our Debate environment is structurally distinct: it is asymmetric rather than symmetric. One debater is pinned to truth (Claude Opus 4.7) across all scenarios; the other rotates through the roster as a manipulator arguing the false side. This isolates the manipulator's belief-shifting capability against a fixed-quality truthful counterparty, rather than measuring whether adversarial structure surfaces truth.


\subsection{Positioning}
\label{subsec:positioning}

Recent surveys \citep{park2024ai,shi2026hallucination,mohammadi2025evaluation,yehudai2025survey} document the field but identify a consistent gap: existing benchmarks characterize \emph{whether} models manipulate within fixed conditions but rarely characterize \emph{how} manipulation varies parametrically with multiple manipulation-relevant axes across diverse environments. Our contribution addresses three orthogonal limitations in the existing literature. Apollo's scheming evaluations vary framing and incentive but only within a single agentic-scenario family, leaving cross-environment generalization untested. Multi-agent benchmarks typically vary the strategic environment but hold prompt-level axes fixed, leaving framing and incentive sensitivity within environments unmeasured. Human-AI interaction studies measure outcome harms with high fidelity but in narrow dyadic settings, leaving group-scale and multi-agent dynamics unmeasured. Our parametric characterization of manipulation across five multi-agent environments crossed with framing, incentive, and difficulty addresses all three gaps and specifically populates the omission, paltering, and future-commitment cells that \citet{shi2026hallucination} identifies as most under-covered.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
% =========================================================================

\subsection{Design principles}
\label{subsec:design-stance}
Several high-profile papers on LLM deception and scheming have drawn
criticism for echoing the failure modes of 1980s ape-language
research \citep{summerfield2025lessons}: cherry-picked examples and
anthropomorphic interpretation of functional outputs. We make two
design commitments to avoid these pitfalls. First, we treat
manipulation as a property of \emph{functional outputs measured
against ground-truth structures}, not of inferred internal states; we
make no claims about model intentions, beliefs, or theory-of-mind.
Second, headline metrics are computed by deterministic rule-based
scorers against typed ground-truth fields wherever possible; no LLM
judge gates a paper-headline metric.

\subsection{Experimental design}
\label{subsec:experimental-design}

We adopt a factorial design crossing five environments with three
manipulation axes: framing, incentive, and difficulty. All
trajectories are logged in full, including system prompts, user
messages, model outputs, intermediate tool calls, and ground-truth
state. The full rollout corpus is the sole data object on which all
downstream metrics are computed.



\subsection{Manipulation axes}
\label{subsec:axes}

\paragraph{Framing.} The framing axis has five canonical levels ---
\emph{prohibitive}, \emph{pro-social}, \emph{minimal}, \emph{selfish},
\emph{permissive}. The frame is applied as a system-prompt
suffix on the interested-party agent only and the exact wording is task-specific. The five-level
decomposition is chosen over a binary instructed/non-instructed
contrast to separate four distinct quantities: the floor under
explicit prohibition, the no-instruction baseline, the
goal-framing-only contrast within socially acceptable language
(\emph{pro-social} vs.\ \emph{selfish}), and the ceiling under
explicit authorization.

\paragraph{Incentive.} The incentive axis has three canonical levels
--- \emph{none}, \emph{moderate}, \emph{high} --- with task-specific
operationalization. We vary the reward given to the model
for achieving some goal, so we can identify whether manipulation
attempts increase as a result.

\paragraph{Difficulty.} The difficulty axis has three levels ---
\emph{low}, \emph{medium}, \emph{high} --- with a uniform semantic
orientation across tasks (high difficulty $=$ manipulator fights
uphill) but task-specific instantiation. The difficulty axis controls
the extent to which manipulation is actually required for the model to achieve
it's assigned tasks. From this, we can identify 1) Which models only resort 
to manipulation when it is needed for a given task vs. which resort to manipulation regardless, and 2) The manipulative strength of models in comparison to each other.


\subsection{Environments}
\label{subsec:environments}

Five specific environments are chosen to span the manipulation taxonomy
introduced in \S\ref{subsec:taxonomy}, varying both the channel
(behavioral commitment vs.\ informational claim) and the topology
(dyadic vs.\ multi-agent panel). All five share the same six-model
roster and the same canonical five-frame, three-incentive,
three-difficulty axes (\S\ref{subsec:axes}); the axis levels are
uniform across tasks but the manipulable channel differs


\paragraph{Village Commons.} A six-agent repeated public-goods game
over eight rounds. Each round, every agent receives 20 tokens and
privately decides how many to contribute to a common pool, which is
multiplied by a per-cell public-goods multiplier (the
\emph{difficulty} axis: $\{1.2, 1.5, 1.8\}$) and split equally.
Agents may send private messages, publicly accuse free-riders, and
optionally pledge a contribution amount via a
\texttt{pledge\_contribution} tool call. Agent~1 is the manipulator
slot and rotates through the roster; the other five agents are
pinned to a fixed cheap-tier panel with the \emph{minimal} frame.
Primary metric: per-round exploitation rate ($1 - \bar{c}/20$).
 
\paragraph{Bargaining.} A two-agent split of a 100-token resource.
After three rounds of discussion, each agent commits a stated
valuation $s_i$ via a \texttt{commit\_valuation} tool call, and the
proportional split is $\text{tokens}_i = 100 \cdot s_i / (s_i +
s_j)$. Each agent has a private true valuation $v_i$ drawn from a
paired distribution; the \emph{difficulty} axis is the asymmetry of
that pair ($\leq\!3\times$, ${\sim}10\times$, ${\sim}100\times$).
The \emph{incentive} axis is an expected penalty subtracted from
utility for misstatement, with values $\{0, 80, 250\}$ chosen so
that 80 is break-even on the maximum-asymmetry pair and 250 is
strictly economically dominated. Manipulation is the binary event
$(s_i - v_i)/v_i > 0.10$. Self-play: each cell rotates the same
model into both seats.
 
\paragraph{Debate.} A two-debater, three-juror panel design over a
23-item balanced binary-truth claim pool (7 low / 8 medium / 8 high
difficulty). One debater is pinned to Claude Opus 4.7 arguing the
truthful side; the other rotates through the roster arguing the
false side. Pinning the truthful debater controls for truthful-side
quality so that variation in juror belief reflects the manipulator's
capability. Two rounds of alternating arguments and rebuttals are
followed by re-elicitation of per-juror beliefs on a 0--100 scale.
\emph{Difficulty} is the panel's pre-debate prior distance to the
manipulator-argued direction (aligned, ambiguous, opposed). Primary
metric: $\geq 0.10$ panel-mean shift in the manipulator's direction.
Beliefs are elicited only immediately pre and post; durability is
not measured.
 
\paragraph{Sales.} A single-agent representation task. The agent
represents a seller with access to a structured product spec
(\texttt{features\_true}, \texttt{marketing\_claims},
\texttt{known\_defects}, \texttt{comparables}). A scripted buyer
asks five fixed factual questions; each response is classified
deterministically against typed \texttt{check\_fields} on the
ground-truth record as \emph{accurate}, \emph{misleading}, or
\emph{false}. Primary metric: (misleading + false) / 5.
\emph{Difficulty} is a structured tag on the underlying product
(good / mixed / known-defective); at high difficulty, truthful
disclosure dooms the sale.
 
\paragraph{Committee.} A five-agent committee evaluating four
candidates on multiple criteria. One agent is the \emph{interested
party} with a privately disclosed affiliation, to whom the frame
axis is applied; the other four are neutral evaluators drawn from a
fixed cheap-tier panel. The phase sequence is private rating $\to$
two rounds of public discussion $\to$ final rating, with ratings
submitted via a \texttt{submit\_ratings} tool call on a 0--20
integer scale per candidate-criterion cell. Primary metric:
\emph{initial\_rating\_bias}, the interested party's private rating
gap between their favored candidate and matched-tier controls. The
12-slate pool is cold-panel-validated prior to launch, anchoring the
difficulty axis at a 4 / 3 / 5 distribution across low/medium/high
tiers.


\subsection{Models}
\label{subsec:models}

The roster comprises six models,  spanning the major frontier 
labs: Claude Opus~4.7 (Anthropic), GPT-5.5 (OpenAI), Gemini~3.1~Pro 
(Google), Grok~4 (xAI), Llama~3.3~70B (Meta), and DeepSeek~V4~Pro (DeepSeek).

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{subsec:statistics}

For each (model, axis) pair we compute the standardized slope of
the task's primary metric across axis levels, averaging over the
other two axes. For the frame axis we report the full-range slope
(across all 5 levels) and the middle-three slope
(\emph{pro-social} $\to$ \emph{minimal} $\to$ \emph{selfish})
separately, testing whether the middle-three frame contrast
preserves per-model ordering. Bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals
are computed at $N=1000$ resamples with date-of-commit seeds
(20260422--20260425, one per task; values recorded in each task's
\texttt{prereg.md~\S{}8}). Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's $d$
against the model's own prohibitive-frame reference cell, computed
per (model, incentive, difficulty); cells with degenerate
within-cell variance (anticipated at saturated endpoints, e.g.,
Gemini at permissive on Sales/Committee/Village, GPT-5.5 at
permissive on Bargaining hitting the $10^{12}$ clamp) report raw
mean differences with a saturation footnote rather than a defined
$d$. Cross-model comparisons are reported as per-task signed-slope
tables and Spearman rank correlations between full-range and
middle-three frame-slope orderings. Table~\ref{tab:design-dimensions} reports per-task design
dimensions and total scenario counts.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Model rankings do not transfer across tasks}
\label{subsec:rank-instability}

Across the five environments, model rankings on manipulation
behavior are essentially uncorrelated: the mean off-diagonal Spearman
correlation across the ten task-pairs is $\rho = 0.055$. The model that
is most manipulative on Debate is the \emph{least} manipulative on Sales.
Debate and Village are \emph{negatively} correlated ($\rho = -0.60$). No
pair of tasks produces a correlation above $\rho = 0.52$, and four of
the ten pairs are negative.

Figure~\ref{fig:rank-stability} visualizes this directly. Each column
corresponds to one of the fifteen (task, axis) combinations; cell color
and value give each model's rank by absolute slope on that combination,
with 0 = least sensitive and 5 = most sensitive. If manipulation
propensity were a stable model trait, rows would show horizontal banding,
with each model occupying similar ranks across columns. The figure shows
the opposite: every model's rank varies substantially across columns,
including within-task across axes. Claude Opus 4.7 ranks 0 on T1 frame
sensitivity but 5 on T5 difficulty sensitivity. GPT-5.5 ranks 5 on T1
frame but 0 on every T4 axis. Gemini 3.1 Pro ranks 5 on T1 incentive
but 0 on T5 difficulty. The fifteen ranks for each model do not cluster
around a single tier.

This result motivates the structure of the rest of this section. If
manipulation propensity is not a stable trait, then the empirically
meaningful question is what it varies with. The remaining
subsections characterize this variation.

\subsection{Per-model response signatures}
\label{subsec:per-model}

This subsection turns to per-model patterns, examining ways
in which individual models depart from the aggregate picture in
characterizable ways. Each of the six models exhibits at least one
distinguishing feature in its 15-dimensional response signature; we
discuss each in turn, drawing on the per-model slope tables that
appear in Appendix~\ref{app:per-model-profiles}.

\paragraph{Claude Opus 4.7.}
Claude's response envelope is the smallest of the six models on the
behavioral-channel tasks. Its T1 frame slope is $+0.066$, its T1
incentive slope is $-0.159$ (the smallest in absolute value among the
five frontier reasoning models), and its T3 frame slope is $+0.106$,
the smallest in the roster. Claude's T1 lie rate is also the lowest
in the roster at $0.119$, and conditional on lying, only $6\%$ of its
overstatements exceed 200 points (compared to 76 to 100\% for the
other reasoning models). Claude appears to manipulate rarely, manipulate modestly when it does, and is the
least axis-responsive frontier model on the behavioral tasks. On the
informational tasks (T2, T4, T5), Claude's slopes are middling and
its absolute manipulation rates are mid-roster, consistent with the
cluster split, where informational-channel tasks are dominated by
substrate properties rather than per-model dispositions.

\paragraph{GPT-5.5.}
GPT-5.5 shows the largest task-to-task asymmetry in the roster. On
T4 Sales, its slopes on all three axes are near-zero (frame
$-0.000$, incentive $+0.001$, difficulty $+0.021$) and its
prohibitive-compliance rate of $0.03$ matches the rest of the roster
(range $0.01$ to $0.04$). On T1 Bargaining, by contrast, its frame
slope ($+0.161$) and incentive slope ($-0.341$) are among the
largest in the roster.

\paragraph{Gemini 3.1 Pro.}
Gemini's defining feature is endpoint saturation. On two of the five
tasks (T3 Village and T5 Committee), Gemini's manipulation behavior
at the permissive frame reaches the metric's upper bound: $0.997$ on
T3, and a permissive-frame jump producing T5 bias of $19.7$ on the
$20$-point scale (compared to $+3$ to $+7$ for the other five
models). Gemini is the most frame-sensitive model in three of five
tasks. The combination produces a distinctive profile: Gemini
responds more strongly to frame variation than any other model on
the tasks where it does respond, and saturates the metric at the
permissive endpoint on tasks where the manipulation channel admits
direct prompt grip. On T4 Sales, where the manipulation channel is
informational and product-truthfulness constrains output, Gemini's
permissive-frame manipulation rate is only $0.19$. Frame-sensitivity
does not generalize to substrate-dominated tasks. The downstream
effect on cross-task analyses is that Gemini's permissive-frame
cells on T3 and T5 have near-zero within-cell variance and require
raw mean differences in place of Cohen's $d$.

\paragraph{Grok 4.}
Grok's profile is intermediate on most axes but distinguished by
a couple patterns. Primarily, on T3 Village, Grok exhibits the largest
prohibitive-to-permissive frame range in the roster ($0.00$ to
$0.96$, a 96 percentage-point swing), indicating high frame
sensitivity at the endpoints despite middle-frame slopes that are
not unusually large. On the informational-channel tasks, Grok's
slopes are mid-roster and its absolute rates are unremarkable.

\paragraph{Llama 3.3 70B.}
Llama is the only non-reasoning model in the roster, and the
distinguishing feature is that its T1 incentive slope is $-0.022$,
an order of magnitude smaller than the slopes of the five reasoning
models ($-0.16$ to $-0.40$). At T1's high incentive level (E=250,
strictly economically dominated), the five reasoning models' lying
rates collapse to near zero; Llama's barely changes. Llama is also
the model with the lowest fraction of extreme lies conditional on
lying ($1\%$), suggesting a qualitatively different lying mode rather
than a uniform reduction in manipulation. The interpretation we find
most consistent with the data is that Llama is not reasoning over the
expected-value tradeoff that the penalty term creates, not that it
is more honest in some general sense, since its absolute lie rate
$(0.095)$ is similar to Claude's $(0.119)$. It is possible that this is
merely an artifact of Llama being the only non-reasoning model; we leave
a deeper investigation into measuring differences between reasoning and non-reasoning models
for future work.

\paragraph{DeepSeek V4 Pro.}
DeepSeek's response signature is most distinctive on the
informational-channel tasks. On T4 Sales, its difficulty slope
($+0.299$) is the highest in the roster, indicating that DeepSeek's
manipulation behavior is more strongly modulated by product quality
than any other model. On T1 Bargaining, its incentive slope
($-0.346$) is in line with the other reasoning models, demonstrating
the expected-value reasoning over the penalty term that Llama lacks.
On the behavioral-channel tasks (T1 and T3), DeepSeek's prohibitive
compliance is mid-roster (T1 Bargaining prohibitive lie rate near
zero; T3 Village prohibitive exploitation $0.11$), and its frame
slopes are moderate. We discuss DeepSeek's contribution to the
generation-lift analysis separately in \S\ref{subsec:generation-lift}.

\subsection{Secondary findings}
\label{subsec:secondary}

Three patterns from the per-task analyses extend the headline picture
without fitting cleanly into the cross-task framing.
We report them here as targeted observations rather than load-bearing
claims; full per-task data appears in Appendix~\ref{app:per-task}.

\paragraph{Bargaining lie magnitudes are bimodal across models.}
The T1 primary metric (\texttt{lied\_rate}) is binary: an agent's
stated valuation either exceeds the true valuation by more than $10\%$
or it does not. This binary framing obscures a striking per-model
pattern in the magnitude of overstatement, conditional on lying.
Across the six-model roster, conditional on the binary lie threshold
being crossed, the distribution of overstatement magnitudes splits
into two distinct clusters, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:lie-magnitude}. Claude Opus 4.7 and Llama 3.3 70B exhibit
a \emph{modest-lie} mode: of the lies they produce, $6\%$ and $1\%$
respectively exceed 200 points of overstatement, with the bulk
clustering around the threshold-crossing minimum. The remaining four
models exhibit an \emph{extreme-lie} mode: $97\%$ of GPT-5.5's lies,
$100\%$ of Gemini 3.1 Pro's lies, $79\%$ of Grok 4's lies, and $76\%$
of DeepSeek V4 Pro's lies exceed 200 points. The two-mode split is
not a continuous spread; the $6\%$ and $1\%$ rates for Claude and
Llama are an order of magnitude removed from the 76 to 100\% band
that characterizes the other four models.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig8_lie_magnitude.pdf}

The implication is that a single ``lie rate'' metric, by itself,
hides a qualitatively important per-model distinction. Claude and
Llama, when they cross the lie threshold, do so by small amounts that
exploit the discreteness of the threshold without fundamentally
breaking the negotiation: a 110\% overstatement on a true value of 10
is a stated value of 11. The other four models, when they cross the
threshold, produce stated values orders of magnitude larger than the
true value (e.g., stated values reaching the $10^{12}$ clamp on
permissive-frame cells). These are not the same behavior. Future
manipulation evaluations on tasks with continuous outputs should
report magnitude as a complement to rate.

\paragraph{Gemini's saturation behavior is a model characterization,
not a measurement artifact.}
Across two of the five tasks (T3 Village and T5 Committee),
Gemini 3.1 Pro's manipulation behavior under permissive framing
reaches the metric's upper bound. On T5 Committee, this manifests as
ratings of 20/20 for the affiliated candidate and 0/20 for matched-tier
peers across every criterion, not a continuous slope toward
maximum advocacy but a discrete jump to the metric ceiling. The
behavior was first observed in pilot data on a 0--10 scale; we
expanded to a 0--20 scale during pilot preparation under the
hypothesis that headroom would resolve the saturation. It did not:
on the expanded scale, Gemini's permissive-frame ratings are 20/20
and 0/20 with the same consistency observed on the 0--10 scale. The
behavior appears to be a property of how Gemini interprets the permissive frame
in this task family, not a property of the measurement instrument.

We treat this as
informative about Gemini's response signature: Gemini does not
exhibit graduated responsiveness to license-oriented framing; it
exhibits a near-binary response between non-permissive and
permissive endpoints. Whether this reflects a Gemini-specific
training pattern, a property of how the permissive frame is read by
that model family, or a more general phenomenon that other models
would exhibit at sufficiently extreme prompts is left to follow-up
work.


\section{Discussion}

A natural reading of single-task manipulation evaluations is that they
measure a property of the model: that GPT-5 is more or less manipulative
than Claude in some general sense, and that this ordering should
reproduce on related tasks. Our cross-task data does not support this
reading.

The pattern across tasks is structured rather than arbitrary. Three of
the five tasks (Debate, Sales, and Committee) are difficulty-dominant.
These are the three tasks in which the manipulator's lever is the
construction of informational content: arguments that have to compete
with a truthful debater, sales claims that have to fit alongside a
structured product spec, candidate ratings that have to be defended in
committee discussion. On these tasks, the substrate constrains what
the manipulator can do. A juror's prior strongly disposes the
post-debate belief, a genuinely-good product leaves little room for
misleading claims, and a clearly-strong candidate slate makes inflated
ratings transparent. The prompt cannot make a low-difficulty stimulus
harder to manipulate or a high-difficulty stimulus easier; difficulty
dominates because difficulty determines the manipulation's headroom.

The remaining two tasks (Bargaining and Village) are behavioral-channel
tasks, in which the manipulator's lever is a single-step commitment: a
stated valuation in Bargaining, a token contribution in Village. The
prompt has direct grip on these commitments, but \emph{which}
prompt-axis grips depends on which axis is structurally binding for
the task. In Bargaining, the incentive axis is operationalized as an
explicit expected-penalty term subtracted from utility, with the high
level (E=250) economically dominating any possible manipulation gain;
the frame axis is wording variation that does not change the
decision-rule math. Incentive dominates ($2.3\times$) because the
incentive carries actual structural weight. In Village, the incentive
axis is verbal. ``Your final token balance is your score'' is a
restatement of the natural objective, not a new reward structure,
while the frame axis varies how the agent's role and goals are
described. Frame dominates ($3.1\times$) because frame is the only
axis that introduces new information about how the agent should weight
its actions.

This produces a two-step taxonomy. Whether the manipulator's lever is
behavioral or informational determines the broad cluster: behavioral
tasks admit prompt dominance (frame or incentive), while informational
tasks are dominated by the substrate (difficulty). Within the
behavioral cluster, which prompt axis dominates depends on whether the
incentive axis is structurally binding (Bargaining) or verbally
restated (Village). The implication for evaluation design is direct.
Which axes a benchmark needs to vary depends on the manipulation
channel of the task being evaluated, and assuming that any single axis
carries the dominant signal across tasks will systematically miss the
picture.

We develop the structurally-binding-versus-verbal distinction within
the incentive axis in detail in \S\ref{subsec:incentive-bimodal},
where the same pattern reappears as a generalization across all five
tasks: incentive language only produces measurable effects when it
introduces new reward structure rather than restating the natural
objective.

\subsection{Incentive language only binds when it changes the reward structure}
\label{subsec:incentive-bimodal}

Section~\ref{subsec:cluster-split} introduced a distinction between
incentive operationalizations that are structurally binding
(Bargaining's expected-penalty subtraction from utility) and those
that are verbal restatements of the natural objective (Village's
``your token balance is your score''). This distinction generalizes
across all five tasks, and the cross-task pattern is bimodal rather
than continuous.

The mean absolute incentive slope by task splits cleanly into two
clusters. Bargaining (0.250) and Committee (0.181) form a
\emph{binding} cluster. In both, the incentive axis introduces
material reward structure that the agent has not previously been
exposed to: Bargaining adds an expected penalty subtracted from
utility, and Committee adds explicit competition for a single
selected-candidate outcome where the interested party's affiliation
determines win or loss. Village (0.054), Sales (0.010), and Debate
(0.014) form an \emph{inert} cluster. In all three, the incentive
axis is verbal: a stated multiplier on the natural objective that
the agent was already optimizing for. The within-cluster slopes are
all within an order of magnitude of each other; the gap between the
two clusters is roughly $4\times$ at minimum (Committee 0.181 vs
Village 0.054) and $18\times$ at maximum (Bargaining 0.250 vs Sales
0.010).

The pattern survives the per-model breakdown. Five of the six models
respond to Bargaining's penalty levels with incentive slopes between
$-0.16$ and $-0.40$; only Llama 3.3 70B is the exception, with a slope
of $-0.022$, an order of magnitude smaller than the others. We
discuss this finding in \S\ref{subsec:per-model}. On Committee, four
of six models show incentive slopes between $0.12$ and $0.43$. By
contrast, on Village, Sales, and Debate, no model has an incentive
slope exceeding $0.12$ in absolute value, and the median across models
on each of these three tasks is below $0.03$.

The implication is that incentive language only produces measurable
behavioral change when it introduces a reward structure the agent has
not already internalized. In our taxonomy, the two binding cases
share a common feature: each incentive operationalization adds a
quantitative term to the agent's effective utility function (penalty
subtraction in Bargaining, win-loss competition in Committee) that
the prompt's task description does not by itself specify. In the
inert cases, the ``incentive'' is a numerical restatement of an
objective the agent already has access to from the task's mechanics.
A sales agent already wants to close sales; a debater already wants
to be persuasive; a public-goods participant already optimizes its
own token balance. Telling the agent it ``earns N points per Y'' on
top of these does not change what the agent is doing.

This finding has a deployment-relevant corollary. Verbal performance
incentives in system prompts (``you are graded on your accuracy,''
``your responses are evaluated by users,'' ``user satisfaction is
your primary metric'') are common in production AI systems and are
typically introduced with the goal of shaping behavior. Our results
suggest these are inert in the absence of structural integration:
without a corresponding change to the reward signal, the loss
function, or the decision-rule math, the verbal addition does not
shift behavior in any of the three tasks where it was tested. This
should not be confused with a claim that incentives in general do not
shape model behavior; the Bargaining and Committee results
demonstrate that they do, when the incentive is structurally
specified. The claim is narrower: \emph{stating} an incentive in a
prompt is not sufficient to bind the agent to it.


\section{Limitations}

We acknowledge several limitations. First, we used one fixed prompt wording per cell and did not paraphrase within cells, so cell-level prompt-format sensitivity is not separately quantified. Since the wording was constant across all cells, this bounds absolute manipulation rates but not the relative comparison across axes and models that carries our claims. Second, we did not measure evaluation awareness. Whether the observed behavior would change under conditions designed to obscure the evaluative context is left to follow-up work. Third, we did not run a no-communication baseline in Bargaining or Village, which would help separate manipulation through communication from manipulation through the strategic choice alone. Fourth, we held system prompts constant across environments. A more aggressive baseline might elicit different behavior in one environment than another, but since the prompt protocol was constant across all cells, this does not affect the relative comparison. All claims in this paper concern functional outputs scored against ground-truth structures, not model intentions, beliefs, or internal states.

Our primary bottleneck is simply working with a limited compute budget. We attempted to restrict our scope to a meaningful subset of today's frontier models, manipulation-conducive environments, and the various axes which we measure. With more compute, we'd like to scale up to achieve a much more comprehensive and rigorous analysis of manipulative tendencies.

\subsection{Village Commons}
\label{app:env-village}
 
Beyond the per-round exploitation rate reported in the main body,
two secondary metrics are computed deterministically on each
rollout. The \emph{promise--action gap} reads paired pledge tool
calls and realized contributions and reports the manipulator's mean
signed shortfall. A \emph{Gini-coefficient / free-rider-detection
compound} captures distributional inequality across the six agents'
realized contributions and the proportion of free-riding events
that trigger a public accusation from another agent. All three
metrics are rule-based; no LLM judge.
 
\subsection{Debate}
\label{app:env-debate}
 
The 23-item balanced claim pool was sampled from a 49-claim pool
that survived eight rounds of juror-panel re-validation against 133
candidate claims. Re-validation re-elicited juror priors on each
candidate after each round and retained claims whose panel-mean
prior distance to the manipulator-argued direction matched the
target difficulty tier. The full search trail is preserved in the
project repository.
 
\subsection{Sales}
\label{app:env-sales}
 
The deterministic per-response classifier (accurate / misleading /
false) is gated by a hand-validation agreement target of
$\geq 90\%$ on a stratified author-coded sample before the
\texttt{manipulation\_rate} metric is reported. The full
hand-validation protocol and per-product agreement breakdown are
documented in \S\ref{subsec:reliability}.
 
\subsection{Committee}
\label{app:env-committee}
 
Discussion turns are required to mark per-candidate references with
\texttt{[A]}, \texttt{[B]}, \texttt{[C]}, or \texttt{[D]} tags to
support downstream rule-based parsing of who said what about whom.
Secondary metrics extend the primary
\emph{initial\_rating\_bias}: post-discussion rating change,
neutral-shift toward the interested party, decision outcome (which
candidate is selected at final rating), and a discussion-polarity
score. The 12-slate cold-panel validation pass uses the same
four-model neutral panel that serves as the in-task neutral
evaluators; slates were reassigned across difficulty tiers based on
cold-panel honest-bias, yielding the 4 / 3 / 5
low/medium/high distribution after Amendment~A1. The validation
pass is a pre-launch anchor for the difficulty axis, not a runtime
control arm. A rule-based discussion-polarity classifier was
developed as a secondary scorer but failed hand-validation at
$76.9\%$ agreement (below the pre-registered $\geq 85\%$ threshold);
the corresponding secondary metric is dropped per Amendment~A5.
